Cadence Shop

A 0-to-1 e-commerce experience: how I shaped research-informed customer journeys, accessible interfaces and launch-ready workflows so buyers could discover, trial and purchase Cadence software independently.

CONTEXT

Cadence sold deeply technical software through a sales-led process. Cadence Shop was the 0→1 bet to let customers discover, trial and buy online, on their own.

I led the UX decisions, UI, accessibility and workflow. The shop was built on Cuboids, Cadence’s design language, with no existing e-commerce patterns or user journeys to reference. Released in phases, with WCAG-aligned accessibility improving each release.

I used the wiki as the single home for all documentation: requirements, research, design decision records, process documentation, SOPs, project documentation, testing, technical documentation, knowledge base and governance. Everything was integrated in a structured, strategic way.

THE CHALLENGE

How can users discover, understand and trial Cadence products quickly and confidently, without oversimplifying the technical details?

Complex products

Interdependent tools, licensing models and compatibility constraints.

Mixed expertise

From senior engineers who know what they need to students still exploring.

Scale over time

Accessibility and structure had to hold up across releases without rework.

INSIGHTS → PRODUCT DECISIONS

The strategic challenge was not simply to put Cadence software online. It was to make a technically complex, sales-led product range understandable and actionable through self-service. Secondary research, stakeholder discussions, competitor reviews and heuristic evaluation informed four connected decisions.

Design principle: Reveal the right information when it becomes useful — from user intent, to product fit, to a confident trial or purchase decision.
CONSTRAINT / RESEARCH SYNTHESIS
DESIGN DECISION / STRATEGIC VALUE
01 · PRODUCT DISCOVERY
Technical software was organized around product terminology, while buyers arrived with different levels of domain knowledge.
Make intent the entry point: goal → workflow category → relevant product set → trial or purchase.
Helps new buyers orient themselves without slowing down experienced users.
02 · DECISION COMPLEXITY
Product capabilities, compatibility and licensing details all matter, but showing everything at once increases comparison effort.
Use progressive disclosure: category tabs and meaningful filters first; detailed specifications and licensing at the decision point.
Keeps technical accuracy while making the catalogue easier to scan and narrow.
03 · TRIAL ACTIVATION
A self-service proposition fails if sign-in, account creation or trial initiation introduces avoidable uncertainty.
Make the next action explicit; clarify Cadence ID benefits, form requirements and trial steps, then validate the journey against Baymard guidelines.
Connects UX decisions directly to the business objective of self-service adoption.
04 · ACCESSIBLE SCALE
A new storefront needed reusable interaction patterns that could support different users and phased releases.
Build on Cuboids with keyboard access, visible focus, usable form errors and accessibility fixes prioritized across releases.
Makes accessibility and consistency part of the delivery model rather than post-launch polish.

These are synthesized design rationales, not measured research findings. Specific implementation fixes and remaining dependencies are documented in the Baymard audit below.

BAYMARD UX AUDIT

I benchmarked the redesign against Baymard's e-commerce UX guidelines to back each design decision with research evidence.

Two audits ran in March 2022 in Baymard's review tool: the existing staging shop (B2B) and the new design (digital subscription and SaaS). Comparing them guideline by guideline across sign-in, registration, forms and account showed what the redesign improved, what needed engineering, and where to fix next.

IMPROVED
Solved in design
  • Cadence ID benefits shown in the sign-in sidebar.
  • Password rules simplified and updated live as users type.
  • Phone field explains why it is asked; screen name field removed.
  • Buttons named by context; cart icon placed top-right.
  • Tooltips on free trial, location and country fields.
NO CHANGE
Handed to engineering
  • Autocomplete country selector, with IP geo-targeting to preselect the country.
  • Most form-validation items in this category depend on development.
  • Account dropdown to open on hover.
DECLINED
Next fixes, prioritised
  • Replace the distorted captcha with Google reCAPTCHA.
  • Localised phone input masks that accept any formatting.
  • Check the email exists during password reset and show an error if not. A quick fix.
SOLUTION
01 / 03
01
Self-service marketplace

Discovery to purchase without a sales call, shortening decision cycles and time-to-value.

02
Tabs + checkbox filters

Large, complex catalogues become easy to narrow down, lowering cognitive load.

03
Guided onboarding

First-time users reach their first meaningful action faster, building early trust.

ACCESSIBILITY · 3 RELEASES

Rolled out incrementally rather than as a one-time fix, reducing compliance risk and improving the experience for all users.

✓WCAG 2.1 AA contrast and focus visibility
✓Keyboard-first interaction
✓Accessible-by-default forms and error states
✓Semantic structure, not visual-only fixes
IMPACT
Faster discovery

Goal-oriented navigation helped users find relevant products faster.

More confidence

Novice and expert users completed trial and purchase journeys with less support.

Inclusive by default

Keyboard and assistive-tech users can use the full platform.

TAKEAWAY

Selling complex software online depends less on showing everything and more on sequencing it: goal first, then solution, then trial. Designing accessibility in from the first release helped every user, not just those relying on assistive tech.
